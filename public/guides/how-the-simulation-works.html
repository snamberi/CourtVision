How the simulation works

Court Vision never invents a box score. Every game is played one possession at a time: someone gets the ball, makes a decision, and the play ends with a shot, a turnover or a foul. Points, rebounds and assists are counted from those events, so a player's statistics come from how he plays, not the other way round.

One possession, step by step

Each trip down the floor runs through the same chain of decisions. Changing a player's ratings, tendencies or minutes changes what happens at each step, and the statistics follow.

  1. Who has the ball. The engine picks a ball handler from the five players on the floor, weighted by each player's ball-handler priority. The weighting is steep rather than linear, so a true go-to star gets a much bigger share of touches than a role player, as usage concentrates on top options in real basketball. Priority is a setting you can edit, not something fixed by position.
  2. What he tries. The possession becomes an action: an isolation, a pick-and-roll, a post-up, a drive, a catch-and-shoot look or a transition chance. Passing tendencies decide whether the ball moves; about a third of assisted shots come from a swing pass by someone other than the original ball handler.
  3. Does he keep the ball? Before any shot, the engine checks for a turnover (see below).
  4. The shot. If there is a shot, the engine chooses one of 14 shot types from the shooter's tendencies, rolls how well it is contested, and resolves the make or miss.
  5. Blocks, fouls and free throws. A shot can be blocked, can draw a foul, or can be an and-one. Free throws are shot one at a time with the shooter's own free-throw ability.
  6. The rebound. A miss goes to a rebounder chosen from all ten players.
  7. Fatigue. Everyone on the floor gets a little more tired, and players on the bench recover.

Fourteen shot types

A jump shot from the corner and a dunk are different skills, and Court Vision rates them separately. Each shot type reads the matching rating, so a great corner shooter is not automatically a great pull-up shooter.

GroupShot types
At the rimRim, close, layup, dunk
Mid-rangeMid-range, long mid-range, fadeaway, post shot, hook
Three-pointersCorner three, above-the-break three, pull-up three, catch-and-shoot three, step-back

Which type a player takes comes from his shot tendencies. You can edit them, or set a target number of three-point attempts, and the engine respects that instead of overriding it.

Contests: open, light, contested, heavy

Every shot gets one of four contest levels. The defender's contest and closeout ratings push the roll toward tighter coverage. In the baseline tuning, roughly a quarter of shots are open.

The make chance depends on the shooter's ability for that shot type, the contest, his fatigue, and whether it is clutch time or the playoffs. Season shooting percentages are never used as an input: they are a result.

Fouls follow the contest too. A heavily contested shot draws a foul about three and a half times as often as an open one, and shots at the rim draw fouls far more often than threes. A disciplined defender fouls less.

Turnovers are opportunities, not a position tax

A turnover is checked on each chance to lose the ball, using the ball handler's handle, ball security and decision making against the defender's perimeter defense and steal ratings, plus pressure from double teams and fatigue.

Because the model works per opportunity, an elite point guard turns the ball over less per touch than an average player. A point guard is not penalised just for having the ball more.

The engine tracks 13 kinds of turnover, including bad passes, steals in the passing lane, on-ball strips, charges, travels, double-team turnovers, shot-clock violations and illegal screens.

Rebounds, blocks and height

  • Rebounds compare offensive rebounding for the shooting team with defensive rebounding for the defense. Defenders get a positioning edge, tuned so the league-wide offensive rebound rate lands near the NBA's roughly 25%.
  • Blocks use the defender's block and rim-protection ratings. Height past seven feet adds a little extra reach.
  • Height matters, but it can never carry a player: it contributes at most about two points of Overall rating.

Fatigue, minutes and injuries

  • Fatigue builds every possession a player is on the floor, faster on hard possessions. Stamina and durability slow it down and speed up recovery on the bench. A tired player's ratings are multiplied down, so a gassed star really does shoot worse.
  • Each player's minutes can be left to the AI, given a target, set exactly, or planned by hand. The substitution planner works toward those totals over the game.
  • Injuries are rolled per possession but kept rare, tuned so a full 82-game season gives each team a plausible handful, not one every game. Some quick modes, such as Survival, play with injuries off.

Badges and ratings above 99

Badges change ratings or switch on special rules, and the engine reads them all the same way, without a special case for each badge. See the badges guide for the full list.

Some modes let ratings go past 99. On the court, every point above 99 counts half, so a 120 shooter is the best ever but not automatic.

Same seed, same game

Every game is driven by a seeded random number generator. The same seed and the same rosters and settings replay the same game, possession for possession. This is how the shared daily and weekly challenges give everyone the same games, and how a saved game can be replayed on the court exactly as it happened.

Make it your own

League settings expose many of these dials: shooting variance, turnover and foul frequency, era rules such as the three-point line, injuries and more. Sandbox mode unlocks experimental badges and editing for testing ideas. New to the game? Start with How to Play.
